---
title: Ads are Absurd
---

As part of my work I watch a truly ridiculous number of political ads. If you look at some of my [work page](https://parmet.com/jeffrey/work/) you’ll see the more serious elements of that, but for my writing I won’t do any kind of real breakdown.

What I do want to talk about is the growing absurdity that comes when you have too much money and are fighting for very limited attention. Between [AI](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/16/us/politics/midterm-elections-ai-generated-ads.html), [loosening restrictions on spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Republican_Senatorial_Committee_v._FEC), [taxpayer-funded ads](https://thehill.com/regulation/court-battles/6244361-judge-denies-dnc-ad-challenge/), and all of the other social trends which make ads work less effectively, we’re in a true wild west of lies and embarrassing depictions.

[This ad](https://host2.adimpact.com/admo/viewer/5665fbf4-ead8-4e54-8906-5c4530e74459), for instance, was a $60k campaign to convince Missouri voters that AI-generated Fauci was coming after your child’s state fair experience.

[This ad](https://host2.adimpact.com/admo/viewer/97dd96a4-f5cd-4317-86a3-c54a1234a15b) is $150k strawmanning the Tumblr era woke liberal by a dairy inheritance millionaire [who lost his primary](https://ballotpedia.org/Jim_Oberweis).

And [this ad](https://host2.adimpact.com/admo/#/public/individual/a109f5e4-f78e-4122-8c96-3c70cfd8b2aa) is FIVE MILLION DOLLARS trying to implicitly call Jon Ossoff gay because it’s important to have a little class when you represent the eighth largest state in the country.

I have a lot of opinions about how to reform political advertising, but no simple regulation is going to fix the way that fear-mongering and mud-slinging is rewarded in our political system.

There are solutions. Overturning Citizens United is one step, as is introducing ads that require transparency in digital advertising the same way television ads are required to be transparent (and maybe modernizing those requirements too).

But the solutions in reach don’t solve the underlying issues. Some of the broader ones are pie-in-the-sky, but if the goal is to 1) help voters make an informed choice and 2) not inundate them with this kind of crock media you have to look more broadly at the incentive structure in American politics.

Good governance is boring, and it’s hard to get folks to buy into boring content. That’s such a huge contributor to the success of Donald Trump. He’s an entertainer, and a pretty good one, even if it does require debasing himself and his audience.

Civics education is the beginning, here. Let people under 18 vote for youth representation, do better about involving students in their local governance—school boards, city councils, park commissions—and let the federal stuff become part of a broader curriculum.

Responsive representation is another. Parliaments in other countries are volatile, yes, but that can be a blessing too. Multi-party coalitions, ranked choice voting, snap elections. All of these can lead to a government that’s more responsive in policy.

And, of course, the election cycle. Between primaries, generals, and the $10+ billion political ads market, voters are inundated for months in a trend that has only moved more and more towards unending political ads. A blackout on political ads until six weeks before an election is a pretty blunt idea, but it does move in the right direction, maybe.

I don’t know what cocktail of policy needs to happen for us to stop putting [AI Putin](https://host2.adimpact.com/admo/viewer/fcd18a6d-439d-4a78-aaed-c13c3fee7a07) in our ads or claiming that a candidate wanted [free Rolexes for all](https://host2.adimpact.com/admo/viewer/df40fd22-eb79-4d10-bf38-ba76b6000614), but I do know that because this kind of regulation is boring, it takes a lot more force to move policy. Maybe what we need is a [flamethrower](https://host2.adimpact.com/admo/viewer/81848932-36fe-4511-9889-853ab5436c76).
