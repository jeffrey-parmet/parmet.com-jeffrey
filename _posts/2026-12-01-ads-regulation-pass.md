---
title: Can Ads Regulation Pass?
---

After I posted Ads are Absurd, my friend prodded at the idea I wrote about in the last line:

> ... I do know that because this kind of [ad] regulation is boring, it takes a lot more force to move policy.

His point was that it’s an issue a lot of people can relate to, and that reducing spam ads is a popular idea. I don’t disagree, though I'm going to outright name the elephant in the room that most of the policy that you could get everyday voters to rally behind in a real way is blocked by the Citizens United decision (more on that later).

While I do think there’s a world where a candidate runs on a digital regulation platform and succeeds—whether that’s Bernie’s anti-PAC platform which has now [reached the DNC](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/11/24/dnc-2028-dark-money-pledge-01181462) or Europe’s GDPR and child safety regulations—moving policy is different than being elected on an issue.

## What does digital ad regulation look like?

If you read the first part of this article and expected legislation to look like a blackout period, I will direct your attention to the [Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act](https://ballotpedia.org/Bipartisan_Campaign_Reform_Act), signed into law in 2002.

The law prohibited national parties, federal candidates, and officeholders from soliciting certain types of contributions in federal elections. It also barred corporations and unions from spending on issue advertisements, which mention a candidate, within 60 days of a general election or 30 days of a primary.

You might have learned about this bill in your history class, because in 2010 it was overturned in [Citizens United v. FEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens_United_v._FEC), which infamously reaffirmed the idea put forward in Buckley v. Valeo that money is speech. For some more info, read [this from the Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/citizens-united-explained).

So if we can’t pass a constitutional amendment or pack the Supreme Court to the point that we can overturn these rulings, our best option is to regulate what we do have and not ban it entirely. This isn’t a new idea. The Honest Ads Act, first introduced in [2017](https://www.congress.gov/bill/115th-congress/senate-bill/1989) and more recently reintroduced in [February 2023](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/486), has a few goals:
Requiring ad disclosure by digital platforms for any advertiser which spends more than $500 in a year.
Applying the same disclaimer rules which exist for traditional ads to digital ads.
Placing an onus on ad platforms to reasonably investigate and limit foreign interference via ad purchasing.

The Honest Ads Act hasn’t been passed, or even left committee. Senator Amy Klobuchar has been a lead introducer in near-identical [press](https://www.klobuchar.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/2023/2/klobuchar-graham-warner-introduce-bipartisan-legislation-to-improve-transparency-and-accountability-of-online-political-advertising) [statements](https://www.klobuchar.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/2022/9/klobuchar-graham-warner-introduce-bipartisan-legislation-to-improve-transparency-and-accountability-of-online-political-advertising).

## Why hasn’t it passed?

If it has bipartisan support, has been introduced multiple times, and has even been incorporated into [H.R. 1](https://www.billtrack50.com/billdetail/1001906) (a symbolic first resolution that the majority party in the House puts up each Congress) at least once, what’s stopping it from passing?

Facebook was actually on the record [in favor of this legislation](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/381990-zuckerberg-announces-support-for-regulating-political-ads-on-social-media/):
> Facebook CEO Mark Zuckerberg announced support Friday for legislation that would regulate political ads on internet platforms, including his own ... "Election interference is a problem that’s bigger than any one platform, and that’s why we support the Honest Ads Act. This will help raise the bar for all political advertising online," Zuckerberg said in a post.

But of course, [one megacompany does not make an industry](https://electionlawblog.org/2018/klobuchar-accuses-tech-companies-of-stalling-honest-ads-bill/):
> "Gathering enough support to get the bill enacted is ‘hard when a lot of the companies don’t want it done,’" Sen. Amy Klobuchar (D-Minn.) said Jan. 30. "That’s our problem."

> "I’ve endorsed it publicly and we’ve implemented it into our systems even though it hasn’t become law," Zuckerberg replied. Unsatisfied with that answer, Klobuchar pressed further on what Zuckerberg has done to encourage Congress to make it law. "Have you done anything to get it passed? Because we’re at a roadblock on it," she responded. [[Politico](https://www.politico.com/news/2020/10/28/klobuchar-tech-hearing-433271)]

Klobuchar [summed it up](https://subscriber.politicopro.com/article/2018/01/klobuchar-honest-ads-act-unlikely-to-pass-this-year-508732) in a statement at a New America event:
> The chances of the bill passing this year are small. But the more you push on it, maybe it gets the [Federal Election Commission] to act, maybe more people come on board. ... You can't say we don't have a chance of doing it this year because either Mitch McConnell doesn't want to or the companies don't want us to, you just have to keep pushing.

Of course, the FEC is in a [sorrier state than it used to be](https://legis1.com/news/fec-nominees-election-commission-decline-super). Money is speech is power, and when there isn’t enough momentum and force behind binding legislation it’s not going to pass.

## Can it Pass?

Legislation only moves if there’s enough advocacy and attention in its favor to overcome lobbying against it. For some bills like banning congressional stock trading, clear corruption makes it connect with voters on both ends of politics.

The impacts of lacking digital regulation are pretty subtle, to the average voter. The Honest Ads Act is not about banning political ads, it’s about making them more transparent. Great for my continued employment and for anti-corruption efforts, but not solving the spam. The type of regulation we need was passed in 2002 and overturned eight years later in a way that makes it particularly hard to reassert (without court packing, at least).

What this means is that the legislation we can pass is limited in scope and doesn’t directly help the people who are inundated with political ads. Transparency regulation, anti-AI regulation (I’ll write a piece on that too), and more are all in the cards and all, indirectly, helpful. The motivation to pass it was strongest in 2016, right after Russia had blatantly interfered in our elections, and that motivation has only faded since. Sen. Klobuchar did not reintroduce the legislation in the last Congress, and it seems unlikely that she will again without prompting.

The focus on corruption has also shifted, even within campaign finance. Congressional stock trading looks like it will be banned soon (hurrah!), as linked earlier the DNC is trying to get candidates verbally committed against Super PACs, and deepfake regulation may also be in the cards. But it seems that the giant burning eye of government regulatory eyes is off digital transparency regulation, for now.
